"Smart people who give a shit use tools"

strong signalessayculture
  • iankduncan.com · Nov 19, 325 HN points on Nov 25
  • Responds to Justin Michael's "we need smart people who give a shit"
  • Core claim: LLMs amplify whoever sits at the keyboard

What it is. A rebuttal to the "AI robs engineers of the struggle that makes them care" argument. Duncan's strongest point: caring was never limited by willingness — it was rationed by how fast one human could read and type. Cheap rigor (specs, property tests, exhaustive caller traces) is what the tool unlocks for people who already care; the meat-proxy failure mode is real but is a hiring problem, not a tool problem.

Why it matters now. This is the best-articulated version of the debate that will keep showing up in client retros and hiring conversations. It also matches this coverage history: every reliable-delivery lane here has landed on "the differentiator is the verification loop, not the typing."

Buildn / client use. Quote the "rationed by how fast one human could read and type" line in pitches. Structurally, Buildn OS's review-gate design is the institutional version of this essay: make caring cheap to enact.

  • Amplifier, not acid
  • Caring was rationed, not rare
  • Hiring problem ≠ tool problem

118 FPS on iOS Safari without flags

try todayfront-end
  • rida.dev · Nov 20, 453 HN points Nov 25
  • WebKit caps rAF + touch events at 60 Hz on 120 Hz screens (flag-gated)
  • Trick: read scroll position synchronously from the compositor thread via OffscreenCanvas placeholder
  • Result: 118 FPS avg while dragging vs steady 60 for pointer events; 60 even in Low Power Mode

What it is. A technique (and demo-heavy write-up) for getting compositor-rate input and output on iOS Safari by treating a scrollable region as a high-rate sensor: native bounce, momentum, snapping all come free from the compositor, and the page reads positions synchronously instead of through throttled events.

Why it matters now. PWAs have been stuck a tier below native on iOS for a decade; this is the first broadly-applicable escape that doesn't require a buried dev flag. The author honestly flags the risk: Apple can moot it any release, as they did with a prior haptics library.

Buildn / client use. For any client product with a mobile web surface that "feels laggy": this is a targeted fix worth prototyping on the one interaction that matters most (swipeable sheet, drag handle), not a rewrite. Goes in the design-to-delivery toolkit next to the React Aria sheet work it builds on.

  • Compositor does the physics
  • No flag, no native app
  • Fragile but impressive
Apple can break this at will; treat as a progressive enhancement, never a dependency. Low Power Mode and accessibility motion settings still interact.

Button Sounds: 50+ multi-sampled UI clicks, free and on npm

delightfulcraft
  • buttons.joshwcomeau.com · 864 HN points, Nov 25
  • 50+ sounds, up to 16 recorded samples per sound; vanilla JS + React APIs
  • npm: button-sounds · self-hosting docs included · agent-friendly docs

What it is. Josh Comeau's library of professionally recorded press/release samples (mechanical keyboards, gamepads, toggles) with random sample selection, pitch variation, and an optional "lofi" filter — a small masterclass in making digital interactions feel physical.

Why it matters now. Sound is the most neglected dimension of web product feel, and the implementation docs (vanilla JS, React, self-hosting) make adoption a half-hour task. Also a quietly great example of AI-policy-aware developer docs — the site explicitly welcomes agent integrations.

Buildn / client use. One candidate: tasteful interaction sound in client dashboards where feedback currently lives entirely in visual toast. Ship with a mute-by-default toggle and respect prefers-reduced-motion-style settings.

  • Free, npm, self-hostable
  • Instant product feel upgrade
  • Well-documented for agents

pw-loudness-eq: Windows' loudness DSP, reverse-engineered for Linux

worth exploringoss
  • github.com/xoxorwr/pw-loudness-eq · 335 HN points Nov 25
  • Port of WMALFXGFXDSP.dll loudness equalization + AudioEng.dll limiter to PipeWire
  • Per-app processing, GTK3 toggle app, C under Makefile

What it is. A faithful reverse-engineering of a Windows-only audio feature that Linux users have asked about for years: even out volume across apps so quiet and loud playback sit at similar levels, with a limiter to keep the boost from clipping.

Why it matters now. For the daily mixed-audio life (calls, videos, music, voice agents), loudness normalization across sources is a real quality-of-life feature — and the repo doubles as a case study in protocol/DSP archaeology worth emulating: measure the black box, port the behavior, prove parity with offline file processing.

Buildn / client use. Direct personal install on the Linux workstation if calls and media coexist; otherwise it's a pattern reference for "reverse-engineer the annoying proprietary behavior" work.

  • Real DSP, faithfully ported
  • Per-app loudness on Linux
  • Clean module split
